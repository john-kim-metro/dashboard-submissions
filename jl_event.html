01 — The Offer

A flyer, a Saturday afternoon, and a discount with strings attached.

The April 18 event ran one afternoon, two locations: Flushing and Fort Lee. The mechanic was a tiered discount on Jeuveau and Letybo units — $10/unit for new patients, returning patients (last visit ≥ 1 year), and existing patients bringing one new friend; $8/unit for patients bringing two or more new friends. List price for both is $12/unit.

The structure was deliberately referral-weighted: discounts deepen the more new patients enter the door. That's not a generic price cut — it's an acquisition mechanic dressed as a discount.

02 — The Audience

Who we sent it to, and why those segments.

The campaign targeted all patients with a recent visit history — anyone who had been to Metro Laser between January 2024 and February 2026, regardless of whether they were actively booking. The target spans both cosmetic and medical (dermatology) histories, biased toward female patients in the prime neuromodulator age range.

03 — The Day

April 18 was, by every metric in our dataset, an outlier.

The practice averages roughly $1,475 in daily Jeuveau/Letybo charges across these two locations and 5 patient encounters. On April 18 it logged $24,820 in charges and 96 encounters — a 17× lift on revenue and a 19× lift on visit volume against the typical day. Restricted to Saturdays only (a fairer baseline) the lift is 34× on revenue and 38× on patient flow.

The previous all-time single-day record in 16 months of data was $5,085 on July 2, 2025. Event day exceeded it by nearly 5×.

04 — New Patients

The acquisition mechanic worked. 9× the daily baseline, 2.6× the prior record.

Both locations saw 23 new patients on April 18 across all services — 13 at Fort Lee, 10 at Flushing. Daily new-patient flow at these locations averages 2.5; the previous all-time daily record was 9 (July 2, 2025). April 2026 closed as the highest new-patient month in the dataset at 72.

An important framing note: this figure includes every new patient across all services that day, not exclusively patients who booked specifically for the Jeuveau & Letybo event. Without patient-level booking-source data, we can't isolate exactly how many of the 23 were event-driven. That said, the 9× lift over typical daily new-patient flow — on a single Saturday — strongly suggests the overwhelming majority were event-attributable. The directional finding is robust.

This is the metric we were most interested in. The pricing structure deliberately rewarded patients for bringing new friends — acquisition was the campaign's core thesis, and it was answered emphatically.

05 — Cannibalization Check

Did we just pull demand forward?

The most natural skeptic's question. If existing patients simply postponed their normal April visit to take advantage of the discount, the post-event week should crash below the prior-year baseline. It doesn't.

Comparing the two weeks before and one week after April 18 to the same calendar windows in 2025: pre-event 2026 was 26% below the same weeks in 2025 (some patients may have waited), but post-event 2026 ran 24% above 2025. New-patient flow held steady in both windows. The data doesn't support a meaningful pull-forward effect.

06 — Channel Attribution

SMS did the heavy lifting. Email is essentially asleep.

The clearest operational finding in this analysis. Of 2,746 attributed scans and link clicks, SMS produced 1,610 — and of 118 inbound calls, SMS produced 110. Social media reached 44,114 people for $504 and contributed 1,074 link clicks but only 4 direct calls. Email's 0.065% engagement rate is roughly 27× weaker than SMS on the same campaign.

A note on attribution: these figures reflect only one of the attribution paths available to a patient — whichever channel they happened to be on at the moment they scanned, clicked, or called. The real patient journey involves many touches: a patient may have seen the social ad, then received the SMS, then mentioned it to a friend, then called. The numbers below should be read as a minimum level of interaction from each channel, not a complete picture of influence.

07 — The P&L

$504 in. ~$10,000+ in net profit out.

Our data lacks product cost per unit, so the gross-profit math uses an estimated $4.50/unit wholesale cost (typical for Jeuveau/Letybo). At that assumption, event-day gross profit is approximately $12,771. After the $504 ad spend and incremental staff time (not yet quantified), net profit on a same-day basis is on the order of $10,000+.

Two cost categories sit outside the $504 figure and deserve flagging. Incremental staff hours for the event — additional injector, front-desk, and support time across both locations — are not included. They're shown as an indicative bar in the waterfall below without a specific figure pending an actual estimate. Platform fees for SMS (Vital Interaction) and email are excluded as marginal cost, since these platforms are paid monthly with unlimited usage and would have been paid for regardless of the campaign.

Note: this analysis omits a "typical Saturday baseline" subtraction because the event ran during hours when these locations are normally closed. Effectively, none of the day's revenue would have happened in the absence of the event.

08 — The Discount, Examined

Did the discount itself actually pay for itself?

The headline P&L answers "did the campaign generate profit." A separate question is whether the discount mechanic itself — $10/unit and $8/unit instead of $12 list — was a smart trade. There's a delightfully simple way to answer it.

On a typical day, these two locations together perform about $1,475 in Jeuveau/Letybo revenue, which corresponds to roughly 5 patients. That's the natural baseline of patients we'd see if we did nothing at all.

On April 18, we saw 96 patients. Even under the most pessimistic assumption — that every one of the typical 5 patients would have shown up that Saturday at full price anyway — the other 91 patients are by definition incremental. They came because of the offer; we wouldn't have seen them otherwise.

At our $4.50/unit cost assumption and 27 units per encounter, each incremental patient adds about $133 in gross profit, while each "would-have-come anyway" patient at promo costs about $70 in forgone gross profit. Working through the math, the discount mechanic breaks even at about 34 incremental patients. We had at least 91. The cushion is roughly 57 patients.

Patients on April 18, in context
Three numbers, one chart. The discount paid for itself anywhere above the break-even line. Even our most pessimistic estimate of incremental patients clears it by a wide margin.

This argument is genuinely conservative. It treats every patient who would normally walk in on a regular day as if they showed up at the event at full price (most days, that's not even Saturday — these locations are normally closed Saturday afternoons). And it ignores the meaningful share of "existing" patients who were on the returning-1-year-ago tier — by definition not actively booking, and therefore incremental rather than would-have-come.

For the analytically inclined, the chart below shows the math directly. As the share of incremental patients on event day grows, gross profit gained climbs (solid line) while forgone gross profit from would-have-come patients shrinks (dashed line). The two lines cross at our break-even threshold of about 34 incremental patients.

The break-even, plotted
Two lines, one X-axis. As more of the 96 event-day patients are "incremental" (drawn in by the offer), gross profit gained climbs and forgone gross profit shrinks. They cross at about 34 incremental patients — above that line the discount makes money, below it the discount loses money.

Bottom line: the discount mechanic almost certainly added net gross profit on top of the $12,771 headline figure — likely on the order of several thousand dollars. Quantifying exactly how much requires capturing which pricing tier each transaction qualified under (new / returning / referring) and tagging "last visit date" against each event-day patient — both fields exist in ModMed.

09 — Caveats & What's Next

What we'd want before declaring this the new template.

Open Items
  1. Product cost per unit is an estimate. Replacing the $4.50/unit wholesale assumption with actual ModMed cost data is the single biggest accuracy improvement available — the entire gross-profit calculation hinges on it.
  2. Pricing-tier mix is assumed, not observed. The split between $10 and $8 transactions wasn't captured in the export. Knowing how many patients qualified for the $8 tier (i.e. brought 2+ new friends) would tell us how strong the referral mechanic actually was.
  3. Social media's contribution is understated. Meta Ads logged 991 cosmetic-inquiry-form views — likely a meaningful share of those became bookings via the form path rather than a direct call. Judging $504 of spend on 4 direct calls alone is unfair to the channel.
  4. Patient lifetime value is not yet measured for Metro Laser specifically. A separate study will be conducted on LTV for cosmetic versus medical (dermatology) patients to quantify the long-term value of the 23 new patients acquired. Until then, the day-of P&L stands without LTV credit.

Operational takeaways for the next event: double down on SMS, treat email as a courtesy channel rather than a workhorse, and instrument the social-to-form-to-booking path before the next paid flight so the ad spend gets credit it deserves. Consider repeating the structure quarterly — the data suggests there's no measurable demand pull-forward to worry about.

1

What self-pay patients actually buy

Before touching a single price, the question is where the money is. It is not spread across the fee schedule — it is concentrated in a handful of codes.

2

Is collection the problem?

Short answer: no. Self-pay realization was 97.6% in 2025 — $343,716 charged against $335,440 collected, a gap of $8,276. On the codes that drive pricing it is better still. This section exists to rule collection out before the rest of the document treats price as the only lever.

What this means for the rest of the document

At 97.6% realization there is roughly $8,000 a year available from perfect collection, and most of it sits in a handful of one-off procedures. The pricing gap identified in the next section is fifteen times larger. Both are worth fixing, but they are separate projects with separate owners — and it would be a mistake to let a collections workstream delay the price change.

3

You are paid the same for harder work

A single flat office-visit price is a reasonable answer to a real operational constraint. The problem is not the flat model — it is that the number was set near the value of the simplest visit.

New Jersey runs two schedules, so it needs two charts. The mid-level rate is where almost all the volume actually sits.

That last column is the number a flat price should track, and it is worth being precise about what it means. Because one price covers three levels, the correct price is the volume-weighted average of the Medicare fees for the levels you actually bill — not the fee for any single code. If a category runs half level 3 at $109 and half level 4 at $155, the mix is worth $132; a flat price set at $109 underprices the entire category. As the panel shifts toward level 4 that weighted average rises, even though no individual Medicare fee has changed.

NY established level-4 visits went from 42.2% to 47.8%, NJ established from 41.3% to 54.5%, and NY new patient from 28.0% to 47.4%. Pricing on the 2025 mix therefore understates what the flat rate should be in three of the four categories — by up to 9% — which makes the recommendation that follows conservative on that basis alone. NJ new patient is the exception, drifting 1.9% the other way as volume concentrated into 99203. Worth confirming the overall shift is genuine acuity drift rather than a documentation or coding-education change.

4

Where you sit against the market

Medicare says whether pricing is internally coherent. It does not say what the market will bear. The phone survey answers that — and it points in opposite directions for the two states. The solid diamonds track the schedule in section 5, so any rate you change there moves them here against the market range.

Because New York charges one rate regardless of provider type, its MD and MLP rows move together — both are driven by the single NY rate in section 5. New Jersey's four rows move independently until the two schedules are combined, at which point its MD and MLP rows converge too.

The two markets are not the same market

Queens is a genuinely low-price market. Median established visit is $100, with a ceiling around $150. Anchoring NY pricing to 125% of Medicare would produce $162 — above every disclosed Queens comparable. Medicare overstates what Queens will bear.

Bergen is substantially richer. Median new-patient visit is $249 and established is $155 — well above what Medicare would justify. Medicare understates Bergen by a wide margin. This is where the real headroom is, not New York.

The single most costly structural choice

NJ prices mid-level visits 45% below MD. But 2025 charges show roughly 93% of NJ self-pay visits are billed at the mid-level rate ($73.25 average charge against a $70 MLP / $120 MD schedule — implying a 6.8% MD share). The MD rate is effectively decorative — NJ charges market-floor prices in the wealthier of the two markets.

The market barely differentiates MD from mid-level

Of the comparables that disclosed both, 4 of 5 in Queens and 3 of 4 in Bergen charge identical rates for physicians and mid-levels. Only The Dermatology Center and SCN Dermatology differentiate at all. Metro NY's single-rate structure matches market norm. Metro NJ's two-tier structure is the outlier — and it is the outlier that costs money.

One competitor already solves the timing problem

Jose Fishman, MD quotes a range for new patients ($167–$328) and tells patients the established-visit price is set by the provider after the visit. The post-visit coding model is already running in your own market — it is not exotic.

The New York split has to be built before it can be billed

New Jersey already posts separate physician and mid-level rates, so applying a differential there is a schedule change only. New York bills one rate regardless of who renders the visit, which is why its physician share is an assumption in the table above rather than a measured figure. Before a differential can go live in New York, the front desk needs a way to identify provider type at check-in — otherwise the mid-level rate becomes the default and the physician rate joins New Jersey's in existing only on paper.

The financial stakes are modest either way: moving the assumed New York physician share from 5% to 15% changes the total by about $4,500 on a $120,000 recommendation.

On one price per family, and two data exclusions

The schedule keeps one price per procedure family rather than one per code: first-lesion biopsy is $125 whatever the technique, each additional is $75, benign destruction is $100 at any lesion count, abscess drainage is $150 simple or complicated, and Botox is $300 per site. That flattens some genuine work differences — 10061 lands at 59% of Medicare and 11106 at 71% — but those are the codes with almost no 2025 self-pay volume, so the simplification costs very little while making the schedule quotable from memory.

Two exclusions worth knowing. A single April 2025 charge of $850 on 11103, against a $50 posted rate, is almost certainly a keying error and has been removed from the base. And the premalignant-destruction ladder is now monotonic — $100 first lesion, $10 each additional to 14 ($230), then a flat $250 at 15 or more — so the old inversion where more disease cost less is gone.

Keeping the physician / mid-level distinction

The schedule above unifies the two states but keeps the two provider types apart. That is the opposite of what New Jersey does today, and it matches what New York does. The case either way:

Pricing physician and mid-level visits differently

For keeping them separate
  • Medicare applies its own 15% physician / NPP differential, so a gap is cost-justified
  • The Dermatology Center and SCN Dermatology both differentiate — it is accepted locally
  • Preserves a lever if physician-rendered self-pay volume grows
  • The $30 gap proposed here is far narrower than New Jersey's current 45% haircut
Against
  • Most comparables do not differentiate — 4 of 5 in Queens, 3 of 4 in Bergen charge one rate
  • Adds a front-desk decision point and a source of quoting error
  • New York cannot identify provider type in self-pay billing at all, so the split must be built before it can be applied there
  • Physician visits are a small share of self-pay — roughly 7% in New Jersey, unknown in New York
6

What this is worth

The procedure half of this is less reliable than the office-visit half

Recomputing the same recommendation on 2026 H1 run-rate instead of 2025, the office visit uplift barely moves — it stays within 0.5%. That number is robust. The procedure uplift drops by roughly a third, because 2026 procedure volume is running well below 2025: 10040 is down 63%, 96910 down 72%, 10060 down 48%, 17110 down 15%.

So treat the total as $110k–$120k in additional charges rather than a point estimate, and find out whether the procedure decline is seasonality, a provider change, or something structural before banking the higher figure.

How much volume could you lose and still come out ahead?

NY new patient is the one category with a genuinely thin margin for error at 15% — it is also the smallest increase (18%) and lands within a few dollars of the market median, so the risk is modest. If any category should be watched weekly after go-live, it is that one.

How to read these medians

Mid-level medians rest on four to five observations each and should be treated as directional. The Queens MD-new median of $162 includes Rebecca Baxt-style midpoint treatment nowhere — it is a straight median of eight disclosed values. Bergen's $249 excludes Khorenian as cosmetic-skewed and uses the $250 midpoint for Baxt; including Khorenian at $350 would raise it further, so the figure used in this document is the conservative one.

The two flagged Forest Hills locations matter more than the other non-responses. Both belong to chains whose Bayside and Fresh Meadows siblings quoted exactly $200 / $150, and chain pricing is normally uniform. Adding those two would lift the Queens established median from $100 toward $150 and change the New York recommendation materially — which is why re-contacting them is on the action list rather than filed as a nice-to-have.

Scope and limits. Pricing comparisons throughout use charge rates, not payments collected, so the gaps shown are pure pricing gaps uncontaminated by collection performance; section 2 handles realization separately. Projections state additional charges, with the collections equivalent given at the current 97.6% realization. Uplift is computed on billed units (2025 charges divided by the posted rate) rather than total encounters, so visits that were charged $0 are assumed to remain $0. Volume and charges are calendar year 2025 actuals — 12 complete months, no annualization. 2026 H1 is used only for trend comparison, never as a projection base. Medicare fees are the 2026 schedule, Region 04 (Queens & Bronx) and Northern 01 (Bergen); comparing 2025 collections against 2026 Medicare slightly overstates each gap, by roughly the size of one annual fee update.

Competitor pricing comes from a telephone survey with roughly 50% response in each market — self-reported and unverified, and the non-response is probably biased upward, since the two large chains that did disclose both quoted $200 / $150. Market medians rest on 4–10 observations per category, so they indicate direction rather than precision. Comp file needs cleanup before external use: a phone number sits in Margaret Ravits' MD Est field, Olga Goulko has a duplicate row showing an implausible $50, Jonathan Gold takes insurance only and should be removed from the denominator, and "N/A" (no mid-levels on staff) should be coded separately from blank (declined to disclose).

Good faith estimate obligations for self-pay patients, and the $400 dispute threshold, should be confirmed with compliance counsel — this analysis is not a legal review. Pricing figures are planning estimates, not financial advice.

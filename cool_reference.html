01 · Overview

The procedure at a glance

CoolSculpting (cryolipolysis) is the FDA-cleared, non-surgical fat-reduction treatment that uses controlled cooling to freeze and permanently destroy subcutaneous fat cells in stubborn areas — abdomen, flanks, thighs, arms, bra fat, back, banana roll, and submental (chin/jawline). It is not a weight-loss procedure.

Device generation note
MD operates the original CoolSculpting device, not the newer CoolSculpting Elite. The differences are hardware: Elite has dual applicators that can run two cycles simultaneously (cutting multi-area visit times roughly in half), C-shaped applicators that cover ~18% more area per placement, and a wider applicator-size selection. For MD, this means: (1) all clinical claims about effectiveness, safety, and permanence apply equally, but (2) multi-area visits run sequentially and take longer than at Elite-equipped competitors, and (3) marketing copy should refer to "CoolSculpting®" generically — never "Elite". At least one direct local competitor (Better U Medical Spa in Fort Lee) markets explicitly as Elite-equipped, which is worth being aware of without conceding ground on it.
02 · Benefits & risks

Benefits & potential side effects

Benefits

  • Non-invasive. No incisions, anesthesia, needles, or surgical risk.
  • Zero downtime. Patients return to work, gym, and routine the same day.
  • Permanent. Destroyed fat cells do not regenerate.
  • Natural-looking, gradual. Results emerge over weeks; no obvious "before/after" event.
  • Low risk profile. Skin barrier is unbroken — minimal infection or scarring risk.
  • Targets stubborn fat diet and exercise can't reach.
  • Strong patient satisfaction — clinical-trial scores around 73% after a single visit; 89% after two visits.

Side effects & risks

  • Common & temporary: redness, swelling, bruising, firmness, tingling, stinging, soreness, itching at the treatment site.
  • Numbness in the treated area can persist up to several weeks (typically 1–4 weeks; rarely up to 12).
  • Late-onset pain 3–5 days after treatment as nerve endings recover (described as "zaps").
  • Rare (0.01–0.1%): Paradoxical Adipose Hyperplasia (PAH) — fat cells enlarge rather than shrink, may require surgical correction. Made famous by the 2022 Linda Evangelista case.
  • Contraindicated for patients with cryoglobulinemia, cold agglutinin disease, paroxysmal cold hemoglobinuria, pregnancy, or recent surgery in the treatment area.
Marketing implication
PAH awareness is the single biggest objection in patient research. The Linda Evangelista coverage is now part of the public conversation. Marketing copy should not hide it — patients searching "CoolSculpting safe?" expect an answer. Lead with FDA-cleared, dermatologist-supervised, 11M+ treatments worldwide, and address PAH directly during consult rather than online.
03 · Terminology

Cycle vs. session — the unit that matters

CoolSculpting marketing uses "session" and "cycle" interchangeably, but they aren't the same thing. The distinction matters for how MD prices, packages, and explains treatment to patients — and for how we read competitor pricing.

Definitions used throughout this brief
TermDefinitionTypical scale
Cycle One applicator placement on one spot. The applicator suctions the tissue, freezes for 35–45 minutes, then releases. Treats roughly the area covered by that applicator cup. 1 cycle = 1 applicator on 1 spot, ~35–45 min
Treatment area An anatomical region (e.g., abdomen, flanks, chin). Most areas need multiple cycles to cover them fully — a full abdomen typically needs 4 cycles, flanks need 2 (one per side). 1 area = 1 to 6+ cycles depending on size
Session (or visit) One appointment. Can include multiple cycles done back-to-back. Note: the newer CoolSculpting Elite generation can run two cycles simultaneously via dual applicators; MD's CoolSculpting device is the original (single-applicator) generation, so cycles run sequentially. 1 session = 1–8+ cycles, ~1–3 hours (longer for multi-area on the original device)
Protocol (or treatment plan) The full course of treatment for a patient, usually 1–3 sessions per area, spaced 6–8 weeks apart. 1 protocol = 1–3 sessions over 2–6 months

Why this matters for pricing

CoolSculpting is sold per cycle, not per session. This is how every reputable provider — including Allergan in their official materials — quotes it. When patients see "$800 starting price" or "$319/cycle," that's one applicator placement, not a complete area treatment.

A common patient misunderstanding: they hear "$500 to treat my stomach" and assume that's the total. In reality, a full abdomen typically needs 4 cycles per session, and most patients need 2 sessions for full results — so the realistic spend at MD's $500/cycle rate is closer to $4,000 per area. Setting this expectation honestly during consult is one of the highest-leverage retention moves a provider can make.

04 · Procedure details

What patients want to know

Patient FAQ snapshot
QuestionAnswer
How long does a visit take? Each cycle runs 35–45 minutes (the applicator's freezing time), and the visit length depends on how many cycles are scheduled. At MD, cycles run sequentially: a typical 2-cycle session is 1.5–2 hours, and a 4-cycle full-abdomen session is approximately 2.5–3 hours including setup, post-cycle massage, and brief rest periods between cycles. Note for staff: clinics with the newer CoolSculpting Elite device can run two cycles simultaneously via dual applicators, cutting visit time roughly in half. MD's device is the original generation.
Does it hurt? Generally no. First 5–10 minutes of each cycle feel intensely cold with pulling/tugging from the suction; the area then numbs. Most patients read, scroll, or nap. The 2-minute post-cycle massage is the most uncomfortable part for some. Mild soreness, like a deep bruise, can last days.
How many cycles will I need? Depends on the area. Submental (chin) typically 1–2 cycles. Flanks 2 cycles (one per side). Full abdomen usually 4 cycles. Thighs 2–4 cycles. The provider determines this in consult based on the area's size and the patient's anatomy.
How many sessions? Most patients do 1–3 sessions per area to reach desired results. Smaller areas (chin) often complete in one session; larger areas (abdomen, thighs) typically need 2 sessions. Sessions are spaced 6–8 weeks apart.
When can it be repeated? Same area: 6–8 weeks minimum between sessions to allow the body to clear the treated cells. Different area: can be done in the same visit if applicator availability allows.
When will I see results? First visible changes at 3–4 weeks. Noticeable results 1–3 months. Full results at 3–6 months as the lymphatic system clears destroyed cells.
Permanent? Yes — destroyed fat cells do not regenerate. However, remaining cells can still enlarge with weight gain, so results require stable weight to maintain.
Recovery / downtime? None. Return to normal activities immediately, including the gym. Most side effects resolve within 1–2 weeks.
Who's a good candidate? Within ~20–30 lbs of goal weight. BMI ≤ 30 (≤ 46.2 for submental). Has pinchable, stubborn fat. Realistic about it being contouring, not weight loss.
Is it permanent if I gain weight? The treated cells are gone, but new fat can deposit in the area or surrounding zones. Patients should be told this upfront — it sets honest expectations and supports retention messaging.

FDA-cleared treatment areas

Submental (under chin), submandibular (under jawline), abdomen, flanks, thighs (inner & outer), upper arms, bra fat, back fat, and banana roll (under-buttock). Not calves or ankles. Submental treatment is also FDA-cleared to affect the appearance of lax tissue — a useful selling point for the chin/jawline angle.

05 · Trends

2026 procedure trends

1. The GLP-1 effect — the dominant shift

Patients who started Ozempic, Wegovy, and Mounjaro in 2024–25 are now reaching stable goal weights. Body contouring consultations linked to GLP-1 weight loss are one of the fastest-growing categories in plastic surgery, according to ASPS 2026 trend reports. McKinsey data: 61% of GLP-1 aesthetic patients lost 11–30% of body weight.

For CoolSculpting specifically, this is a nuanced opportunity:

  • GLP-1 patients with residual stubborn fat pockets after stable weight loss are ideal candidates.
  • Patients with significant skin laxity are not — they need surgical lifts or RF skin tightening, not cryolipolysis.
  • The "GLP-1 maintenance + targeted contouring" angle is real and underused in dermatology marketing.

2. Combination protocols are now standard

Single-modality treatment is fading. The 2026 standard is CoolSculpting + Emsculpt (or Emsculpt NEO) + skin tightening — fat reduction, muscle building, and skin laxity addressed together. Many providers now bundle, with strong evidence that Z-Wave or radial pulse therapy after CoolSculpting can nearly double fat loss at 8 weeks.

3. Premium membership models

Beyond per-session pricing, providers (StudioMD, Tribeca MedSpa) are layering in annual memberships ($99–$295/mo) that bundle CoolSculpting at deep discounts with CoolTone, skincare products, and other Allergan treatments — a retention play that converts one-time fat-reduction patients into long-term aesthetic clients.

4. "Refined preservation" over dramatic transformation

ASPS 2026 trend reports describe the year's aesthetic shift toward "refined preservation, regenerative sculpting and natural, anatomy-honoring results." CoolSculpting fits this narrative perfectly: gradual, subtle, no surgical signature.

5. Allergan's national pull-through is strong

Allergan's annual CoolMonth and Allē rewards program are aggressive demand-generation engines. Spring is the peak campaign window, and the program has grown each year (62,000+ treatments performed during a single CoolMonth in 2023). MD, as a CoolSculpting provider, has direct access to this co-op marketing infrastructure. Detailed calendar in Section 15.

The trend that matters most for MD
The GLP-1 wave is the most important trend to internalize. Position CoolSculpting as the finishing tool for GLP-1 success — not a competitor to weight-loss drugs, but the complement that handles what the medication can't (localized stubborn fat). Many MD patients are likely already on GLP-1s; the EHR can identify them once Darell's data-match project completes.

11 · Unit economics

Unit economics & pricing strategy

CoolSculpting has a per-cycle consumable cost that makes its margin structure different from most aesthetic services. Understanding this changes how MD should think about discounting, packaging, and whether to raise prices in 2027.

The consumable model

Every cycle requires single-use materials that the practice purchases from Allergan, plus an Allergan licensing/billing component. This differentiates CoolSculpting from, say, Botox (where the consumable is the toxin itself, sold by unit) or laser treatments (where the consumable cost is essentially zero after the device is purchased).

Per-cycle materials and licensing typically include:

  • Gel pads — placed between skin and applicator; single-use, disposed after each cycle.
  • Applicator liners or sleeves — single-use protective covers on the applicators (U-shaped on the original device generation MD operates; C-shaped on the newer Elite generation).
  • Treatment cards / cycle activation — Allergan's per-cycle licensing mechanism. Each cycle requires activation, and Allergan tracks/bills per cycle delivered.

MD's confirmed cost per cycle is ~$150. At MD's $500/cycle list price, that produces a 70% gross margin per cycle ($350) before staff time and overhead.

16 · Catch phrases

Catch phrases & elevator pitches

Short, memorable framings staff can drop into casual conversation when CoolSculpting comes up — at the front desk, during a medical visit, on the phone. Each one earns its place by being true, specific, and emotionally resonant in under fifteen seconds.

The principle behind a good catch phrase isn't to "sell." It's to give the patient a clean, accurate mental model of the procedure in one sentence so they can make a decision. Avoid superlatives ("amazing," "incredible") — they sound like marketing. Use specifics — they sound like medicine.

The core phrases Both audiences

When asked "What is it?"
"It's the only FDA-cleared way to permanently remove fat without surgery — the device freezes the fat cells and your body clears them out over the next few months."
Best for first-time inquiries. Anchors three facts in one sentence: FDA, permanent, non-surgical.
When asked "Does it actually work?"
"In clinical studies, 89% of patients are satisfied after their second visit. We've seen the same here — most patients are surprised by how natural the results look."
Pairs evidence with social proof. The "89% after their second visit" anchor also subtly establishes that two visits is normal.
When asked "Is it for weight loss?"
"Not weight loss — body contouring. It's for the stubborn fat that doesn't respond to diet and exercise. Most of our patients are already close to their goal weight."
The most important reframe. Setting this expectation early prevents poor-fit consults and disappointed patients later.
When asked "How is it different from a medspa?"
"Every treatment plan here is designed and reviewed by a board-certified dermatologist — not just a technician. That changes what we can spot during the consult and how we adjust your plan."
MD's strongest differentiator. Use without putting down medspas — let the comparison speak for itself.
When asked "Will it hurt?"
"The first ten minutes feel intensely cold, then the area numbs. Most patients read or scroll their phone during the rest of the cycle. Some take a nap."
Honest about the cold. The "some take a nap" detail is disarmingly specific and lowers anxiety.
When asked "When will I see results?"
"You'll start to see change at three to four weeks, with full results at three months. It's gradual — friends notice you look great without knowing exactly why."
The timing matters for booking the consult — they need to understand summer-event planning has to start now, not in July.
When asked "Is it really permanent?"
"Yes — the fat cells we treat don't grow back. As long as your weight stays stable, the contour you see at three months is the contour you keep."
The honest caveat ("as long as your weight stays stable") protects against unrealistic expectations and feels trustworthy.
When the patient is post-GLP-1
"Ozempic does the heavy lifting on overall weight. CoolSculpting is what handles the specific spots that don't respond, even when the rest of your body has changed."
The fastest-growing patient segment in 2026. This phrase positions MD as the next step in their journey, not a competitor to their progress.

17 · Phone scripts

Phone inquiry scripts

Cold inbound phone calls are the highest-leverage moment in the entire patient pipeline — the patient has already raised their hand. The job of the phone call is not to sell the procedure. It is to book the consult and prevent the patient from leaving the call without a date.

The opening Front-desk

The opening sets the tone. A warm, confident greeting is more important than the words themselves.

"Thanks for calling Metro Dermatology, this is [name]. Are you calling about CoolSculpting today?"
Naming CoolSculpting upfront does two things: it confirms you understood why they called, and it signals that this is a normal, frequent inquiry — not something exotic. If the caller hesitates, they may be calling about something else; pivot smoothly. If they confirm, you're already past introductions.

The qualification — three quick questions Front-desk

Before quoting prices or describing the procedure in depth, qualify the lead. Three questions, in this order:

"Great. Just so I can point you in the right direction — have you had CoolSculpting before, or would this be your first time?

And do you have a particular area you're hoping to treat?

And which of our locations is most convenient — Flushing or Fort Lee?"
These three questions accomplish a lot quickly. The "first time" question helps you pitch the right thing (a first-timer needs education; a repeat patient needs a rebook). The "area" question gauges seriousness — vague answers ("I don't know, just whatever") often signal a price shopper. The "location" question subtly assumes they're going to book somewhere — a small commitment that makes the next step easier.

The pivot — from inquiry to consult Front-desk

The most common mistake on the phone is over-explaining. Resist the urge to describe the entire procedure. The patient called to schedule, not to be educated. Move to the consult booking quickly:

"The best next step is a free consultation with our cosmetic team. They'll evaluate the area, design a plan that's specifically for you, and walk you through pricing — which depends on the area and the number of cycles you'll need. Consultations are free and there's no obligation. I have an opening this Thursday at 2pm or Friday at 10am — which works better for you?"
Three things to notice: (1) "free" and "no obligation" lower the perceived friction of saying yes; (2) the pricing question is deflected to the consult — never quote prices on a phone call, since pricing depends on the area and cycle count, and a wrong number sets a bad anchor; (3) offering two specific times is dramatically more effective than asking "when works for you?" The two-option close removes decision paralysis.

Handling the price question Front-desk

This is the question every caller asks and the one most likely to lose them if handled poorly. Never quote a flat price. Never say "it depends" without context.

"It depends on the area and how many cycles you'll need — most patients invest somewhere between $1,500 and $4,000 for a complete treatment plan, but the consultation is what nails down your specific number. We also have a current promotion — buy three cycles and get the fourth free, which most of our patients qualify for. Would Thursday or Friday work better for the consult?"
The range gives the caller a real anchor without committing to a wrong number. The promo mention plants a value reason to act now. Then immediately back to the close — never let the conversation drift after a price discussion.

The price-shopper signal Front-desk

Some callers won't accept the consult and will keep pushing for a precise price comparison. This is usually a price shopper who has already called three other clinics. Don't fight it; redirect honestly.

"I understand wanting to compare. The honest answer is that any clinic quoting a flat price over the phone is guessing — they haven't seen your area or your goals. What I can tell you is our cycles are $500 each, our patients average somewhere in the $1,500 to $4,000 range for a complete plan, and our pricing is comparable to other dermatology practices in the area. The consult is the only way to know what your number actually is, and it's free. Would you like to schedule one?"
Honesty disarms a price shopper better than a sales pitch. Stating the per-cycle rate ($500) gives them a real number to compare, without committing to a total. The "comparable to other dermatology practices" framing positions MD against medspas without naming them.

If they won't book Front-desk

If after all of this the caller still won't commit to a consult, capture them for follow-up rather than letting them disappear:

"Totally understand wanting to think about it. Could I send you a quick text or email with the highlights and our promotion details, so you have it when you're ready? What's the best number for you?"
The opt-in collection here is critical. A "no" today is often a "yes" in three weeks if you can stay in front of them. The text/email choice gives the patient control. Make sure the opt-in is properly recorded so Vital Interaction can include them in future campaigns.
18 · Consult framework

Consult conversation framework

The consult is where the real conversion happens. Done well, it ends with a booked first session and a patient who feels understood. Done poorly, it ends with "I need to think about it" — which is almost always a no. The framework below structures a 30–45 minute consult into seven stages.

Stage 1 — Rapport & framing (3 min) Both

Don't open with the procedure. Open with the person.

"Hi [name], I'm [your name]. Before we dive in, tell me a little about what brings you in today — what made you think about CoolSculpting, and what would feel like a great outcome for you?"
Two open questions that surface motivation and goals. The patient's answer here is the most valuable information you'll get all visit — it tells you which fears to address and which benefits to emphasize later. Take notes if appropriate. Don't interrupt.

Stage 2 — Goals discovery (5 min) Both

Drill into the goals from Stage 1. The patient said something general ("my stomach"). Get specific.

Ask follow-ups: "When you say your stomach, do you mean the lower abdomen specifically, or the whole area?" "Is there an event coming up — a wedding, vacation, reunion — or is this more of a long-term goal?" "Have you tried other approaches — diet, exercise, GLP-1 medications?" The answers tell you whether they're a strong candidate, what timing pressures exist, and what they've already ruled out.

Stage 3 — Candidacy assessment (5–8 min) Provider

Physical evaluation: pinch test, assessment of skin laxity, evaluation of fat distribution and symmetry, BMI consideration, and screen for contraindications (cryoglobulinemia, cold agglutinin disease, recent surgery, pregnancy).

Two outcomes are possible: the patient is a good candidate, or they're not. If not, the consult is over and you say so — kindly. Treating a poor-fit patient is the worst long-term decision a practice can make. It hurts them, it hurts the practice's reputation, and it costs you the referral they'd have given if you'd been honest. If they have significant skin laxity, redirect to Ultherapy or surgical lifting. If they're not yet at a stable weight, suggest they reach goal weight first.

Stage 4 — Setting expectations (5 min) Both

This is the stage that most directly affects retention. The consult is your chance to set the patient up for protocol completion (two sessions) rather than disappointment after one.

"Let me explain how this typically works, so you know exactly what to expect. CoolSculpting is sold per cycle — that's one applicator placement on one spot. For a full abdomen, most patients need four cycles per visit, and most patients do two visits about six to eight weeks apart, for the best result. So we're talking about a complete plan, not a single appointment.

Results emerge gradually — you'll start seeing change at three to four weeks, and your full result at three months. It's not a dramatic moment; it's a gradual shift that you and the people around you notice over weeks."
Three things this script does: (1) introduces the cycle/session/protocol distinction explicitly, which prevents pricing surprises later; (2) normalizes two visits as the standard plan, addressing MD's 84% one-and-done retention problem at the moment of greatest leverage; (3) sets gradual-result expectations, which prevents the "is it working?" anxiety that drives some patients to give up before session 2.

Stage 5 — Pricing presentation (5 min) Front-desk / Coordinator

The transition to pricing should feel natural, not transactional.

"Based on what we've discussed, here's the plan I'd recommend: [X cycles] over [N sessions]. At our standard rate of $500 per cycle, that's [$total]. With our current Buy 3 Get 1 Free promotion, your investment for the complete plan would be [$total with promo].

We do offer financing through CareCredit if you'd like to spread the payment out, and many patients find it easier that way. Do you have any questions about the pricing or the plan?"
Frame the dollar amount as an "investment," not a "cost" — the word matters. Present financing proactively rather than waiting for the patient to ask, which removes a friction point. The closing question invites questions but doesn't push for a decision yet — that comes in Stage 7.

Stage 6 — Question & objection handling (5–10 min) Both

Section 19 covers the specific objections and how to handle each. The framework is: listen fully, acknowledge the concern, address with a specific fact, and end with a question that moves the conversation forward.

Stage 7 — The close (3 min) Front-desk / Coordinator

The most important — and most underused — step. The patient has been educated, qualified, priced, and reassured. Ask for the booking.

"I think you'd be a great candidate, and the timing actually works well — if we book your first session in the next two weeks, your results will be at their peak just in time for [their event / late summer]. I have an opening Tuesday morning or Thursday afternoon next week. Which works better for you?"
Three components: (1) affirmation ("you'd be a great candidate") confirms their decision is sound; (2) timing logic links their goal back to a specific calendar — this is why setting expectations in Stage 4 matters; (3) the two-option close removes the open-ended "when would you like to come in?" question that often produces "let me check my schedule and get back to you" — which is usually code for "no."

If the close doesn't land Front-desk / Coordinator

If the patient says "I need to think about it," that's not a no — it's a request for more information or more time. Don't push, but don't let them leave without a follow-up plan.

"Totally understand. What would help you make the decision? Is it the pricing, the timing, or something else?"
This question surfaces the real objection. "I need to think about it" almost always masks a specific concern — price, fear of pain, partner consultation, scheduling, fear of side effects. Once you know which one, you can address it directly. If they truly need time, schedule a follow-up call: "How about I check back with you on Friday — would that work?"
19 · Objection handling

Objection handling

Most consults end with the same handful of objections. Each one has a recommended response and a principle behind it. The principle matters more than the script — patients can tell when they're being recited at.

"Isn't there a risk of that thing that happened to Linda Evangelista?" (PAH) Both

The most common safety objection in 2026, and the one most likely to surface unprompted. Don't deflect or minimize it. Address it directly and honestly.

"I'm glad you brought that up — it's a fair question. What you're referring to is called Paradoxical Adipose Hyperplasia, or PAH. It's real, and it does occur in roughly one in a thousand to one in ten thousand cases. It's why I want to do a thorough evaluation before treatment, why this needs to be done by a dermatologist-supervised practice, and why we follow up carefully after each session. The honest reason it became famous is that it can require surgical correction if it does occur — but the rate is low, and we've never seen it in our practice."
Three principles at work: (1) name the thing (PAH) — patients trust providers who use the medical term; (2) give the actual rate (1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000) — vague reassurance ("it's very rare") feels like avoidance; (3) tie it back to MD's differentiator (dermatologist supervision) without sounding like a sales pitch. If MD has had a PAH case, modify the last sentence honestly — never lie. Trust is built in moments like this.

"That's more than I expected. Can you do better on price?" Front-desk / Coordinator

The instinct is to discount. Resist it.

"I hear you — it's a real investment. The promotion we have right now is the deepest discount we offer publicly: buy 3 cycles, get 1 free, which is a 25% effective savings. We can also break the payment up through CareCredit so you're not paying it all upfront — many patients prefer that. Would that help?"
Discounting on the spot trains patients (and the staff member) that prices are negotiable. Stay anchored on the promotion as the discount and offer financing as the friction reducer. If a patient genuinely cannot afford the full plan, suggest starting with a single area or single cycle rather than dropping the per-cycle rate.

"I saw [Better U / SKINNEY / a medspa] is cheaper. Why are you more expensive?" Both

Don't disparage competitors. Reframe the comparison.

"That's a fair question. The honest answer has two parts. First, our pricing reflects that every plan here is designed and supervised by a board-certified dermatologist — not a technician. That changes what we can spot during the evaluation, especially around skin laxity, scarring history, or whether you'd actually be better served by a different procedure. Second, we follow up with our patients carefully — we want you back for the second session because that's when the result really comes together. Some clinics treat each session as a transaction; we treat it as a plan."
Two value pillars — clinical expertise and protocol completion — both real differentiators that medspas can't credibly claim. The "not a technician" line lands without insulting medspas (they really are staffed primarily by aestheticians, by design). The "treat it as a plan" line connects price to MD's interest in the patient's outcome, not the transaction.

"They have CoolSculpting Elite — yours is the older model" Both

This will come up. The honest answer respects the patient's research without conceding clinical ground.

"Good catch — yes, our device is the original generation. The clinical results are identical: same FDA clearances, same fat reduction per cycle, same safety profile. The difference is hardware: Elite has dual applicators that can run two cycles at once, so a multi-area visit is shorter at an Elite-equipped clinic. With us, your visit will run about 30 minutes longer for a 4-cycle treatment. The trade-off is that you spend that time with a dermatologist-supervised team rather than a technician working alone. Most of our patients tell us they actually prefer the pace."
The principle here is concede what's true, defend what matters. Don't pretend MD's device is Elite. Don't pretend the visit length is the same. Both are easy to verify and lying creates much bigger trust problems later. Instead: own the difference, name it specifically (30 minutes longer), and pivot to the value MD does deliver. The "most patients prefer the pace" line is supported by general medspa-vs-derm consumer research and feels confident rather than defensive.

"Can I just do one cycle to try it out?" Both

Patients sometimes want to "test" before committing. The provider's job is to set realistic expectations about what one cycle will and won't do.

"You can — but I want to be honest with you about what one cycle does. For a smaller area like under the chin, one cycle might be enough to see a real change. For a larger area like the abdomen or flanks, one cycle covers a portion of the area, and the result will be uneven. Most patients who do one cycle and stop are disappointed — not because the procedure didn't work, but because it wasn't given a chance to. If you'd like to start with one cycle on a smaller area to see how your body responds, that can work. For a larger area, I'd really recommend a complete plan."
Honesty about under-treatment outcomes is one of the most important conversations in cosmetic medicine. Patients who do one cycle and stop become bad-review patients. The framework is: agree it's the patient's choice, explain what one cycle realistically produces, and recommend a different starting point that doesn't set them up to fail.

"I want to wait until after summer / until things calm down" Front-desk / Coordinator

This is usually a soft objection that masks something else. Probe gently.

"That's fair — and it's actually a good reason to start now, not later. Results take three months to fully emerge. If you start in May, you're at peak result by Labor Day. If you wait until fall, you're at peak result for spring — which is great, but pushes your goal back six months. What were you hoping to look forward to?"
The biology timeline (Section 12) is the truth in this conversation. The patient's instinct to "wait" usually comes from believing the procedure has a recovery period or seasonal logic that doesn't actually apply. Reframe the timing around their goal, not around their schedule.

"I need to talk to my husband / partner first" Front-desk / Coordinator

Almost never a real reason. Almost always a soft no driven by another concern — usually price, sometimes fear. Surface the underlying issue.

"Of course — that makes sense. Just so I can give you the right information to share with him, what do you think his main question will be? Pricing, time commitment, the procedure itself?"
Reframe "I need to talk to my husband" from objection to information request. The patient's answer to "what's his main question" reveals what their own hesitation actually is. Once you know, you can address it. If they really do need partner consultation (which happens), schedule a follow-up: "Would it help if I sent you a one-page summary you could share with him? And let's pencil in a call for Friday to check back."

The rebooking script — the most important 30 seconds in the practice Front-desk / Coordinator

This conversation happens at checkout after session 1. Do not let the patient leave without a session 2 booking.

If you wait until you can see today's full result, the window for session 2 is already closing. Let's hold a date — we can always reschedule, but having it on the calendar makes it easier to plan."
The principle: hold the date, even if tentatively. Patients who leave with no calendar entry rarely come back. Patients with even a tentative entry come back at much higher rates because there's a concrete commitment to keep or move. The scheduling itself is what creates retention, more than any single conversation.

Two paths — membership (high-LTV conversion) or three-month check-in (relationship continuation). Either is a win. The check-in option is important because patients who just had a procedure aren't always in the mindset to commit to a membership immediately, but they're easy to convert at the three-month mark when they can see results.
21 · Provider talking points

Provider talking points

Dermatologists are the highest-trust voice in the practice. A single mention from Dr. Lee or another provider during a medical-derm visit can move a patient further than a dozen marketing emails. But the line between professional recommendation and feeling like a sales pitch is real, and the patient can sense the difference instantly. The frameworks below are designed to keep that line clean.

The principle Provider

Don't sell. Notice. The patient came in for something else — a rash, a screening, a follow-up. Their visit is not the time for a CoolSculpting pitch. But it is a fine time for a provider observation that opens a door, leaves it open, and lets the patient walk through if they want.

The line a provider should never cross
Never raise CoolSculpting unprompted with a patient who hasn't expressed body-contouring interest, and never raise it as a comment on the patient's body. Saying "you might want to consider CoolSculpting" to a patient who came in for a mole check is a violation of the provider relationship and will be remembered as an insult, not a recommendation. The provider's role is to respond to patient-raised cues, not to volunteer cosmetic suggestions.

When the patient mentions weight, body, or appearance Provider

If during the medical visit the patient brings up something appearance-related — "I've been struggling with this stomach pooch since the kids," "I've lost a lot of weight on Ozempic but my arms still bother me" — that's a patient-opened door. The provider can respond.

"That's actually something we treat here — we offer a non-surgical body contouring procedure called CoolSculpting that's specifically designed for the kind of stubborn fat that doesn't respond to weight loss alone. Our cosmetic team does free consultations if you ever want to learn more. Otherwise, let's get back to what brought you in today."
The structure: name the procedure, link it specifically to what they mentioned (Ozempic stubborn fat, post-pregnancy areas, etc.), offer the free consult as the next step, and immediately return to the original visit reason. The "let's get back to what brought you in" line is what makes this feel like a recommendation rather than a sale — the provider is signaling that they're not pivoting the visit.

When the patient asks the provider directly Provider

If the patient asks ("I've been thinking about CoolSculpting — what do you think?"), the provider can speak with full authority.

"It's a procedure I've supported for years — it's safe, it's FDA-cleared, and the results in the right candidate are very natural. The key is candidacy. It works best for someone who's at a stable weight with specific areas of stubborn fat — it's not a weight-loss treatment. If you want, I can have our cosmetic team do a free consultation to see if you'd be a good fit."
The provider's voice carries weight precisely because they're not a salesperson. Use plain, honest language: "I've supported this for years," "the key is candidacy," "free consultation to see if you'd be a good fit." Avoid promotional language ("amazing results," "you'd love it"). Hand off to the cosmetic team for the actual sales conversation — the provider's job is the endorsement, not the close.

When the patient is post-GLP-1 Provider

This is the highest-leverage provider conversation in the practice in 2026. Patients on or recently off GLP-1 medications often have stable goal weights with stubborn pocket areas — a textbook CoolSculpting candidate. They also frequently raise it themselves: "I've lost the weight but I still have…"

"Congratulations on the progress with [Ozempic / Wegovy / Mounjaro] — that's significant. The places where you're still seeing stubborn fat are exactly what CoolSculpting is designed to address. The medication does the systemic work; CoolSculpting handles the localized areas the medication can't reach. If your weight has been stable for a few months, you'd be a strong candidate. Want me to have the cosmetic team reach out about a consultation?"
Three things this script does well: (1) congratulates the progress without questioning it — the GLP-1 patient has often felt judged about taking the medication; (2) positions CoolSculpting as a complement to their journey, not a competitor; (3) qualifies on stable weight, which is the actual clinical requirement and feels professional rather than transactional. The "want me to have the cosmetic team reach out" framing is a softer ask than "would you like to book a consult" — it removes immediate decision pressure.

When the answer is no Provider

Sometimes the patient asks about CoolSculpting and they're not a good candidate — significant skin laxity, BMI too high, weight not stable, contraindicated condition. The provider's honest assessment matters more than a booking.

"I appreciate you asking. To be honest, CoolSculpting probably isn't the right fit for your situation right now — [specific reason: you have some skin laxity that would be better addressed with Ultherapy / I'd want to see your weight stable for a few months first / etc.]. The good news is there are other things that would work well — let me have our cosmetic team look at the full picture. The goal here is the right procedure for you, not just any procedure."
Honest no's protect the practice's reputation more than any number of yeses. A patient who's told "actually, this isn't right for you" by a dermatologist remembers that — and tells friends. Misfit consults that turn into misfit treatments turn into bad reviews and refund requests. The "right procedure for you, not just any procedure" line is one of the strongest things a provider can say, and it costs the practice nothing.
The compounding insight — closing thought on Part Four
Marketing and sales serve different functions but share one principle: the patient can always tell. They can tell when a phone script is recited rather than meant. They can tell when an objection response is rehearsed defensiveness. They can tell when a provider's recommendation is a sales pitch in disguise. The frameworks in this section work because they assume the patient is paying attention. Don't memorize the scripts. Internalize the principles, and let the words come from you. That's the difference between a practice that grows on retention and a practice that has to keep buying new patients to stay flat.

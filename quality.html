The Operating Manual Vol. I · No. 02 For Working Reference

Quality, by design.

A pocket reference of process improvement concepts — how to reduce defects, eliminate rework, and stop solving the same problem twice.

Applied To
Revenue Cycle · Patient Flow
Clinical Ops · Admin Workflows
How to use

Most problems that look like capacity problems are actually quality problems. A team drowning in AR follow-up rarely needs more bodies — it needs fewer denials reaching them in the first place. Every prevented defect is permanent capacity gained.

The concepts below are tools for finding causes, not symptoms. They reward patience: the diagnosis usually takes longer than the fix. Use them when you keep solving the same problem and it keeps coming back.

01
Improvement methodologies
Three concepts · Structured frameworks

Six Sigma & DMAIC

Methodology
Define · Measure · Analyze · Improve · Control
What it is

A disciplined five-phase approach to reducing defects and variation. Core insight: most variation is caused by the system, not the people. Targets 3.4 defects per million opportunities (the "six sigma" level).

When to apply

Recurring, measurable defects with significant cost. Best when you have data and a clear definition of "defect." Overkill for small, obvious problems — use for systemic ones.

RCM. Denial rate stuck at 9% costing six figures annually. Define what counts as a denial, measure by reason code, analyze root causes, improve top drivers, control via dashboards. Typical project: 3–6 months, 30–50% reduction.

Lean & the 8 Wastes

Methodology
DOWNTIME: Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-utilized talent, Transport, Inventory, Motion, Extra-processing
What it is

Toyota Production System adapted for any work. Define value from the customer's view; everything else is waste to eliminate. Most service operations are 60–90% waste by elapsed time.

When to apply

Workflow redesigns. Whenever lead time is long but actual work time is short. Especially powerful when paired with value stream mapping.

RCM. A claim's lifecycle: 30 days to payment, but only ~45 minutes of actual work. The other 29 days, 23 hours is waiting (W), rework on denials (D), and handoffs (T). Attack the wait, not the work.

PDCA / Deming Cycle

Methodology
Plan → Do → Check → Act → (repeat)
What it is

The original continuous improvement loop. Smaller and lighter than DMAIC — designed for rapid iteration on small changes. Hypothesis → experiment → learn → standardize or discard.

When to apply

Day-to-day improvements that don't warrant a formal project. Building a culture of experimentation. When the cost of trying is low and you'll learn more from doing than analyzing.

Patient flow. Hypothesis: rooming nurses before provider arrival cuts visit time by 4 minutes. Plan: try with one provider for 2 weeks. Do, check timing data, act on what you learned. One PDCA = one week.
02
Diagnostic tools
Four concepts · Find the cause

The 5 Whys

Diagnostic
Ask "why?" until you hit a system cause, not a person
What it is

The simplest root cause technique. Keep asking why until you reach something structural (process, system, training) rather than personal (someone made a mistake). Five is approximate — usually 3–7.

When to apply

Any time the obvious answer feels too simple. Especially when your first instinct is "we need to remind the team to be more careful" — that's almost never the real answer.

RCM. Why denied? Missing modifier. Why missing? Coder didn't see op note. Why? Op note wasn't signed. Why? Provider waits till end of week. Why? No prompt in EHR. → Fix the EHR prompt, not the coder.

Fishbone / Ishikawa

Diagnostic
6Ms: Method · Machine · Material · Measurement · Manpower · Mother Nature
What it is

A structured brainstorm that forces you to consider all categories of cause, not just the convenient one. Visualizes contributing factors as branches off a central problem.

When to apply

Complex problems with multiple plausible causes. Group root cause sessions where you want everyone to contribute, not just the loudest voice. Pairs naturally with 5 Whys for each branch.

Patient flow. "Why are visits running long?" — could be Method (workflow), Machine (EHR slow), Material (charts incomplete), Measurement (clock starts wrong), Manpower (training), or Environment (room layout). Map all six before fixing one.

Pareto Analysis (80/20)

Diagnostic
80% of problems come from 20% of causes
What it is

A ranked frequency analysis to identify the "vital few" causes that dominate any problem. Almost universally true in operations data: a small number of categories account for the bulk of the volume.

When to apply

Before any improvement project. Deciding what to fix first. Resisting the urge to address every issue equally. Especially powerful when your problem feels "everywhere" — it isn't.

RCM. Denials feel chaotic. Pareto by reason code reveals 7 of 80 codes drive 82% of denials. Two are eligibility, two are coding, one is auth. Five focused fixes > 80 scattered ones.

Value Stream Mapping

Diagnostic
Value-add time ÷ Total lead time = Process Cycle Efficiency
What it is

A detailed map of every step in a process with value-add vs. non-value-add time labeled. Reveals how little of total elapsed time is actual work. Often the single most clarifying exercise in operations.

When to apply

Before automating anything. Before replicating a process at a new location. Whenever lead time and effort feel mismatched. Once per process every 2–3 years minimum.

RCM. Charge to cash: 45 min value-add ÷ 30 days lead time = 0.1% efficiency. Most "improvements" target the 45 min. The 30 days is where the gold is.
03
Analytical lenses
Three concepts · Make sense of data

Statistical Process Control

Analytical
Mean ± 3σ = control limits. Within = noise. Outside = signal.
What it is

A chart that distinguishes normal variation from meaningful change. Saves you from over-reacting to a bad week and under-reacting to a real trend. The discipline of "is this signal or noise?"

When to apply

Any metric you watch over time. Especially denials, no-shows, posting accuracy, days in AR. Replaces "the number went up, panic" with statistical reasoning.

RCM. Denial rate jumps from 4.2% to 5.1%. Is that real? SPC says: if it's within the normal band, no — same process, different week. If outside, something changed. Investigate accordingly.

Common vs. Special Cause

Analytical
Common = system. Special = event.
What it is

Deming's distinction. Common causes are baked into the system (94% of problems). Special causes are one-off events (6%). Fixing them requires different approaches — and confusing them creates chaos.

When to apply

Before "tampering" with a process that's having a bad day. When a single error triggers calls for systemic change (often wrong). When a chronic problem gets blamed on an individual (also often wrong).

Patient flow. One disastrous Tuesday = special cause (a no-show provider). Persistent 30-min waits every Tuesday = common cause (Tuesday is overbooked). Different fixes entirely.

Cost of Quality

Analytical
Prevention + Appraisal + Internal failure + External failure
What it is

A framework that quantifies what poor quality actually costs. Includes obvious costs (rework) and hidden ones (lost revenue, customer attrition). Prevention costs are typically 1/10th of failure costs — but get cut first.

When to apply

Justifying investment in prevention. Building a business case for improvement projects. Showing finance why the "expensive" quality program is the cheap option.

RCM. Cost to resolve a denial: ~$25. Cost to prevent it (better front-end edits, training): ~$3. Multiplied across 8,000 denials/year: $200K vs. $24K. Quality is the cheap option.
04
Preventive design
Three concepts · Engineer errors out

Poka-Yoke (Mistake-Proofing)

Preventive
Make the right way the only way
What it is

Designing processes so errors are physically or logically impossible. Better than training, better than checklists. The classic example: a USB-C cable that can't be plugged in upside down. The system enforces correctness.

When to apply

Errors that recur despite training. High-stakes steps where human error is costly. Any place a checklist is currently the "fix" — checklists are usually a sign poka-yoke would be better.

RCM. EHR template that won't let a claim drop without required modifiers for certain CPT codes. The biller can't submit incorrectly — the system blocks it. One-time engineering > perpetual training.

Standard Work

Preventive
Document the current best known way — then improve it
What it is

The documented, repeatable, current best practice for a task. Not a constraint on creativity — a baseline so improvement is measurable. Without standard work, there's no real process, just habits.

When to apply

Onboarding new staff. Opening a new location (critical — you can't replicate what isn't written down). Any process where outputs vary by who performs it. Foundation for improvement.

Multi-location. New site can't perform like the original if the "how" lives only in tenured staff's heads. Standard work is the asset that lets a second location exist without becoming a different practice.

Failure Mode & Effects Analysis

Preventive
Risk = Severity × Likelihood × Detectability
What it is

A structured exercise to identify how a process could fail before it does, score each failure mode, and address the highest-risk ones. Originated in aerospace; now standard in healthcare quality.

When to apply

Before launching anything new — a new location, EHR module, payer contract, service line. Annual review of high-stakes processes. Whenever "we've never tried it this way" is the situation.

Multi-location. Before opening site #2, brainstorm: what could go wrong with credentialing? Pre-auth handoffs? Cross-site eligibility checks? Score each. Mitigate the worst 5 before opening day.
05
Cultural foundations
Two concepts · Make it stick

Kaizen (Continuous Improvement)

Cultural
Many small improvements > few large ones
What it is

The philosophy that improvement is everyone's job, every day, in small increments. Opposite of the big consulting project. Most powerful improvement engine known — but requires culture, not just tools.

When to apply

Always, in parallel with any other approach. Especially when the team feels like quality is "something done to them" rather than by them. Best signal of kaizen culture: front-line staff suggesting and implementing fixes weekly.

Practice-wide. One billing tweak per biller per month = 60 improvements/year. Even if each saves only 5 minutes/week, the compound effect dwarfs any single project. The hard part is the habit, not the ideas.

Gemba (Go & See)

Cultural
The truth lives where the work happens — not in the report
What it is

The discipline of leaders observing actual work in its actual location, in real time. Reports and dashboards are filtered abstractions. Most leadership "knowledge" of operations is wrong by the time it reaches them.

When to apply

Before any major operational decision. When reports and reality seem to disagree. Regularly, as a leadership practice — weekly is not too often. Sit with a biller for two hours; you'll learn more than from any report.

RCM. Dashboards show AR follow-up is on track. Sit next to an AR rep for an hour: half their time is on hold with payers. The dashboard didn't measure it. The improvement opportunity is invisible from upstairs.

Reach for this, when…

Quality problems disguise themselves as capacity problems, training problems, and personnel problems. The right diagnostic tool is what tells them apart.

"We keep fixing the same problem over and over."

5 Whys + Poka-Yoke

You're treating symptoms. Find the root cause, then engineer the error out so it can't recur.

"Where do we even start? Everything's broken."

Pareto Analysis

It isn't everything. A handful of causes drives most of the pain. Rank by frequency or cost and fix those.

"The metric jumped — is that real or noise?"

Statistical Process Control

Set control limits. Within them = normal variation. Outside = something actually changed.

"Why does this process take a month for an hour of work?"

Value Stream Mapping

The waste isn't in the work — it's in the waiting between steps. Map it to see it.

"How do we make sure the new site performs like the original?"

Standard Work + FMEA

Document the current best way, then proactively identify how it could fail at a new site.

"Quality investment keeps getting cut from the budget."

Cost of Quality

Quantify failure costs (rework, lost revenue) against prevention costs. The math usually wins.

"The big improvement project never gets finished."

Kaizen + PDCA

Stop chasing the big project. Run many small experiments instead. Compound effect beats heroic effort.

"Reports look fine but something feels off."

Gemba Walk

Go where the work happens. Watch for an hour. Dashboards lie by omission; reality doesn't.

"Multiple causes — how do we map them all?"

Fishbone / Ishikawa

Structured brainstorm by category (Method, Machine, Material, etc.). Surfaces causes you'd miss otherwise.

The Operating Manual Vol. I · No. 01 For Working Reference

Capacity, measured.

A pocket reference of operations management concepts — what each one tells you, and the moment to reach for it.

Applied To
Revenue Cycle · Patient Flow
Call Centers · Service Ops
How to use

Capacity problems wear many masks. The same symptom — a growing backlog, a stressed team, a missed target — can come from a constraint, a queue, a variability mismatch, or simply the wrong measurement. Different tools cut differently.

Scan the categories below. Match the question you're trying to answer to the lens that fits. The concept names are the search terms; the examples are how they land in a clinic.

01
Foundational laws
Three concepts · Always true

Little's Law

Foundational
L = λ × W   |   Inventory = Throughput × Flow Time
What it tells you

For any stable queue, the amount of work sitting in it equals how fast work arrives times how long each item takes to complete. Know any two, solve for the third.

When to apply

Any time you're managing a queue with arrivals and exits — claims, patients, calls, tickets. It's the starting point for almost every capacity diagnosis.

RCM. Days in AR climbing? Apply Little's Law: either claims are arriving faster (volume up), being worked slower (cycle time up), or both. The equation tells you which lever to pull.

Theory of Constraints

Foundational
System throughput = Bottleneck throughput
What it tells you

Every system has exactly one bottleneck at a time. Total output is governed entirely by that one step. Capacity added anywhere else is wasted.

When to apply

Before hiring or restructuring. Before automating any one step. Whenever someone says "we need more people" — first ask where, and why that step specifically.

Patient flow. Adding more exam rooms won't shorten visits if check-in is the bottleneck. Adding front-desk staff won't help if the bottleneck is the provider. Find the constraint first.

Queueing Theory

Foundational
Wait time grows as ρ / (1 − ρ)
What it tells you

Utilization above ~85% causes wait times to explode non-linearly. A team running at 95% isn't efficient — it's fragile. Small disruptions create weeks of backlog.

When to apply

When justifying "slack" to finance. When a team that looks fully utilized keeps falling behind. When variability (sick days, payer batches, seasonal spikes) feels disproportionately disruptive.

Call center. Staffing to 95% utilization sounds efficient but produces 5-minute hold times. Staffing to 80% produces 30-second holds. The math is non-linear; intuition undersells it.
02
Diagnostic tools
Four concepts · Measure first

Takt, Cycle & Lead Time

Diagnostic
Takt = Available time / Demand
Cycle = time per unit
Lead = total elapsed time
What it tells you

Takt is the pace you need. Cycle is the pace you have. Lead is what the customer feels. If cycle > takt, you're structurally under capacity — no amount of effort fixes it.

When to apply

Right-sizing a team. Deciding whether a process needs more people or better tools. Comparing target performance to reality without ambiguity.

RCM. 500 claims/day ÷ 5 billers × 7 productive hours = 5.04 min/claim takt. If actual cycle is 7 minutes, you're 40% under capacity — that's a math problem, not an effort one.

Utilization & Cushion

Diagnostic
Target utilization ≈ 80–85% for variable demand
What it tells you

How much of available capacity is actually used. A planned 15–20% cushion absorbs variability, training, meetings, and growth. Not slack — slack is unplanned. This is engineered.

When to apply

Reading idle-time data (like HelloRache). Setting headcount targets. Justifying why "100% busy" is the wrong goal.

RCM. Idle 7% means no cushion — fragile. Idle 64% means overstaffed or misallocated. Idle 15–20% with stable backlog is the sweet spot you're aiming for.

Service Blueprinting

Diagnostic
Map every step → measure → find waste
What it tells you

Where capacity actually goes. Most teams discover 60–70% of staff time goes to a few step types — rework, status checks, exceptions — not the work they think they do.

When to apply

Before automating anything. Before redesigning a workflow. When you suspect the work itself is the wrong shape, not the team size.

Patient flow. Map check-in to checkout. You'll often find a third of the lead time is patients waiting on internal handoffs invisible from any one role's vantage point.

Inventory & Aging Analysis

Diagnostic
Trend backlog volume + oldest-item age over time
What it tells you

Whether throughput is keeping up with demand. Productivity metrics tell you how hard a team works; aging tells you whether that work is enough.

When to apply

Weekly capacity check-ins. Whenever productivity looks fine but outcomes don't. Especially good as a leading indicator before days-in-AR moves.

RCM. If denial backlog is stable at 200 items and oldest is <14 days, capacity is adequate. If either is climbing, you have a problem — even if every biller is "busy."
03
Strategic levers
Three concepts · Design choices

The Pooling Effect

Strategic
Combined variance < sum of individual variances
What it tells you

Combining variable demand streams smooths the combined queue. Cross-trained generalists need less total capacity than the same headcount split into deep specialists.

When to apply

Cross-train vs. specialize debates. Designing teams for a multi-location practice. Whenever one queue spikes while another sits idle.

RCM. The mathematical argument for cross-training: pooled denials + AR follow-up needs ~15% less capacity than the same staff siloed, because spikes in one offset lulls in the other.

Chase vs. Level Strategy

Strategic
Vary capacity  |  vary demand  |  vary backlog
What it tells you

Three ways to handle variable demand: chase it (flex headcount), level it (smooth demand), or absorb it (let backlog flex). Most service ops default to the third by accident.

When to apply

Annual capacity planning. Deciding between PT/PRN staff vs. full-time. Whenever backlog has become a structural buffer instead of a temporary one.

Call center. Most call centers chase demand with shift staggering. RCM teams almost always level capacity and let backlog absorb the swings — fine until backlog becomes permanent.

The Operations Triangle

Strategic
Cost  ◬  Quality  ◬  Speed / Flexibility
What it tells you

You can't optimize all three at once. Where you sit on this triangle is a strategic choice, not an operational one. Pretending otherwise produces incoherent staffing decisions.

When to apply

Before any capacity decision with budget implications. Whenever leadership wants "faster, better, cheaper" — naming the tradeoff is the work.

RCM. Staff for peak claim volume = high cost, fast service, low backlog. Staff for average = lower cost, periodic delays. Both are valid; choosing matters more than the choice.
04
Workforce modeling
Three concepts · Headcount math

Erlang C

Workforce
Staffing required = f(arrival rate, handle time, service level)
What it tells you

For a given arrival rate, handle time, and target service level (say, 80% of calls answered in 30 sec), the precise headcount needed. Small service-level increases require disproportionate staffing.

When to apply

Phone-based teams. Any work queue with measurable arrival patterns. Whenever someone proposes a service-level target without knowing the cost.

Call center. Going from "answer in 60s" to "answer in 30s" can require 30%+ more staff — same calls, same handle time. Erlang C makes the cost of that promise visible.

Driver-Based FTE Modeling

Workforce
FTE = f(volume drivers)  not  FTE = last year + 1
What it tells you

Headcount as a ratio of business drivers (encounters, claims, providers, locations) rather than a function of last year's headcount. Lets you forecast capacity for any future scenario.

When to apply

Opening a new location. Onboarding a new provider line. Building a defensible budget. Replacing "we just need one more" arguments with math.

RCM. 1 AR FTE per ~12,000 annual claims; 1 pre-auth FTE per ~8,000 auth-requiring visits. New location's projected volume × ratios = incremental hire plan.

Learning Curves

Workforce
~50% productivity month 1 · ~75% month 3 · ~100% month 6
What it tells you

New hires aren't full FTEs immediately. Knowledge work ramps over months. Hire-by date and full-capacity date are different dates.

When to apply

Planning hires ahead of growth, not in reaction. Building location-opening timelines. Setting realistic ramp-period expectations with finance.

Multi-location. New site opens June 1, needs 4 FTE at maturity. Hiring May 15 = ~2 effective FTE in month 1. Start hiring March to land at full capacity by month 4.
05
Industry benchmarks
Two concepts · External reference

Published KPI Benchmarks

Benchmark
MGMA · HFMA · HBMA published medians
What it tells you

How your numbers compare to peer practices. Useful as a sanity check; dangerous as a target. Specialty mix, payer mix, and automation level vary enormously.

When to apply

Initial assessment of where you stand. Board reporting. Validating internal targets. Never as a substitute for understanding your own process.

RCM benchmarks. Cost to collect 3–5% (high performer), days in AR <35, AR>90 days <15%, net collection rate 96%+, first-pass resolution 95%+, denial rate <5%.

Productivity Standards per FTE

Benchmark
Work units per FTE per day — by function
What it tells you

Whether individual output is in a reasonable range. Combined with idle %, separates "working hard" from "throughput sufficient."

When to apply

Per-person capacity reviews. Identifying training opportunities. Comparing automated vs. manual workflows.

RCM ranges. Claims submission 80–120/day · AR follow-up 40–60 accounts · Denials 25–40 worked · Payment posting 600–1,200 lines manual · Pre-auth 15–25/day.

Reach for this, when…

The hardest part isn't knowing the concepts — it's remembering which one fits the moment. Match the question to the tool.

"Our backlog keeps growing — what do I look at first?"

Little's Law + Aging Analysis

Decompose into arrivals, cycle time, or both. Backlog age tells you which lever broke.

"Should we hire more people?"

Theory of Constraints

Find the actual bottleneck before adding headcount anywhere else. Most "hiring" requests fail this test.

"Are we right-sized? What's the math?"

Takt vs. Cycle Time

Pace required vs. pace achieved. If cycle > takt, no effort fixes it.

"Everyone's busy but we keep falling behind."

Queueing Theory

Utilization above 85% causes wait times to spike. Busy ≠ adequate.

"Should we cross-train or specialize?"

Pooling Effect

Cross-training reduces total capacity needed by smoothing combined variance.

"How many FTEs for the new location?"

Driver-Based Modeling + Learning Curves

Ratios applied to projected volume, ramped over realistic onboarding time.

"What service level can we afford?"

Erlang C + Operations Triangle

Naming the cost-quality-speed tradeoff explicitly, with math.

"Where does staff time actually go?"

Service Blueprinting

Map the process before redesigning. Most time goes to invisible step types.

"How do we compare to peers?"

Published Benchmarks

Use MGMA/HFMA/HBMA as a sanity check, not a target. Context matters.

Overview

This document takes the raw ticket data and examines the nature of the work being performed — what types of issues are occurring, how technicians are responding, and whether those responses are resolving problems at their root.

The intent is not to dismiss the value of ongoing support, but to have a clear-eyed conversation about where technical hours are being invested, and whether the current pattern of engagement is moving the environment toward stability or sustaining a steady-state of recurring intervention.

Rather than reviewing ticket volume alone, this document goes deeper into the raw data to assess the quality of each resolution — examining whether issues were genuinely closed at the root or simply cleared to reappear in the next cycle.

The analysis covers 1,539 closed tickets and 827 logged technician hours spanning seven months across six locations. Tickets are separated into two categories: server-side infrastructure work and user endpoint issues.

Chapter One
Server Infrastructure — The Case of ADDS-119NY-DC05
01

One server generated 150 tickets over seven months — all driven by a single repeating performance metric. Understanding what was done, and what was not done, tells a broader story about infrastructure management.

ADDS-119NY-DC05 produced the highest ticket volume of any machine in the estate. Of those 150 tickets, 148 were for the same alert: Disk Transfers/sec exceeding a threshold. This happened on average more than 20 times per month for seven consecutive months, with the same small set of causes identified repeatedly.

Key observations
Pattern of Concern
The antivirus scan was "rescheduled" 15 separate times
The same remediation — moving the antivirus scan to off-hours — was applied on November 10, November 17, November 21, December 15, December 18, December 25, January 12 (twice), and continuing through April. Either the configuration change was not being saved persistently, or it was reverting. This is a one-time fix that became a monthly billing event.
Pattern of Concern
56% of tickets were closed without a meaningful fix
83 of 148 disk performance tickets were resolved with notes indicating the alert cleared on its own, was expected behavior, or had already resolved before the technician checked. These tickets represent monitoring activity, not remediation. The alert threshold for this server was never adjusted despite identifying recurring causes.
Infrastructure Observation
Memory pressure was identified and never addressed
Twelve tickets note that the server was "paging due to memory pressure" and that disk activity normalized after the workload finished. Server paging to disk is a hardware signal — the machine does not have sufficient RAM for its concurrent workloads. Each of those 12 tickets closed without a hardware assessment or workload redistribution recommendation.
What Is Working
Legitimate corrective actions were taken on 44% of tickets
65 tickets involved real interventions: backup job timing adjustments, print spooler restarts, chatty service suppression, and storage agent resets. These are valid managed services activities. The concern is not that the work is being done — it is that the underlying conditions driving the alerts are not being eliminated.

Chapter Two
User Endpoints — Recurring Failures Across the Fleet
02

743 tickets were generated by user workstations and laptops across all six locations. Two issue types — Windows Update failures and Group Policy errors — account for 93% of all endpoint tickets.

Unlike server alerts, endpoint issues are often directly felt by staff. A machine that cannot apply patches is a security liability. A machine that cannot read Group Policy may lose access to printers, mapped drives, or security configurations. The volume and recurrence of these issues warrants a closer look at whether the current response strategy is building toward a stable fleet or managing a persistent backlog.

Notable Pattern
WU failures improved steadily then reversed sharply in March
Failures declined from 109 in October to a low of 29 in February — a meaningful improvement. In March, the count rebounded to 72. A spike of this magnitude typically corresponds to a major Patch Tuesday release where a deployed package fails across multiple endpoints simultaneously. No ticket documentation addresses this rebound at a fleet level or explains what caused it.
Root Cause Gap
Machines with corrupted DataStore.edb keep having it corrupted
DataStore.edb corruption is not random — it is typically caused by improper shutdown, low disk space, or hardware issues. Multiple machines had this file replaced across repeated tickets without any follow-up on why it keeps corrupting. Treating the symptom without understanding the cause means the machine will fail again.
Group Policy Errors — 244 tickets over 7 months

Group Policy failures on endpoints are almost never caused by the endpoint itself. When a workstation cannot receive or apply Group Policy, it is typically because something upstream — DNS resolution, domain controller replication, secure channel trust, or Sysvol file availability — has broken. The resolution breakdown below reflects this: the majority of real fixes involved DNS configuration, domain rejoining, or secure channel repairs.

Infrastructure Signal
Machines being rejoined to the domain more than once
Five machines — BXPROVIDER, NYCOSOFF02, NYHLWY06, BXCOSNS01 — required domain rejoining twice within the seven-month period. A domain rejoin is a significant corrective action that should be rare. Its recurrence on the same machines indicates an undiagnosed underlying issue: either network instability, machine account problems in Active Directory, or a domain controller that is not reliably maintaining trust relationships.
Infrastructure Signal
DNS is causing Group Policy failures across multiple locations
35 Group Policy tickets traced back to DNS resolution failures — meaning workstations could not find a domain controller to authenticate against. DNS instability of this scale points to a configuration or infrastructure issue that should trigger a DNS audit. Individual per-ticket DNS fixes are not equivalent to resolving the underlying DNS architecture question.
Chapter Three
The Value Proposition — Where It Holds, and Where It Needs to Grow
03

The data does not suggest that managed services are delivering no value. It suggests the value being delivered is reactive, and that the environment would benefit from a shift toward infrastructure-level resolution.

Value Confirmed
Real technical work is being performed and documented
Named technicians are logging specific actions, referencing diagnostic tools (PerfMon, Resource Monitor, gpresult, wuauclt), and documenting outcomes. The ticket notes show genuine investigation. This is substantively different from a purely automated alert-and-close operation.
Area for Development
Root cause resolution should become a measurable outcome
47 machines accumulated four or more Windows Update failures over the period without a documented root cause resolution. ADDS-119NY-DC05 generated 150 alerts from one metric without a threshold adjustment or hardware recommendation. The shift being requested is from per-ticket response to outcome accountability — tracking whether recurring issues decrease over time as a result of the work being done.
Clarification Needed
Ticket volume discrepancy between report and raw data
The summary report shared in April 2026 cited 838 proactive tickets. The raw ticket data contains 1,539 closed tickets across the same period — nearly double. Understanding how these numbers reconcile, and what categories of work were excluded from the summary report, would help ensure both parties are working from the same picture of the environment.
Bottom Line

Where the managed services contract stands today — and what comes next.

Metro Dermatology maintains an internal IT team as well as a dedicated technician sourced through the same vendor — both of whom handle daily end-user troubleshooting. As a result, the MSP's scope has effectively narrowed to proactive monitoring and remediation, and serving as an escalation point when internal resources require additional support. That is the lens through which the value of this contract should be evaluated.

It is worth acknowledging that not every ticket in this dataset represents a concern. Of the 169 machines that generated tickets over the seven-month period, 35 had issues that were resolved once and never returned — a result that reflects the kind of clean, effective remediation this contract is meant to deliver. That work is recognized and appreciated.

However, those 35 machines account for only 100 tickets — 6.5% of the total volume. The remaining 84% of tickets belong to patterns that repeated on the same machines, often with the same fixes applied. Across the two largest alert categories — Disk Transfers/Sec and CPU/Processor — the repeat rate was 100%. Windows Update issues recurred on 91% of affected machines. Receiving alerts and responding to them is a legitimate part of proactive monitoring — the concern is not that alerts are firing, but that the same alerts, on the same machines, keep firing month after month with the same temporary fixes applied. A proactive posture should produce a declining pattern over time as root causes are identified and eliminated. The data reviewed here does not consistently show that trend.

This review is limited in scope to the managed services contract only. Other engagements with the vendor — including network and security — are independent and remain valued. The question being evaluated is whether the managed services contract, as currently delivered, justifies its cost relative to an alternative: bringing on a dedicated internal IT Operations Manager. Such a role could provide the same proactive monitoring and remediation coverage through an RMM platform, while also taking on infrastructure projects, root cause investigations, and fleet-level improvements that fall outside the boundaries of the current contract — at a comparable annual spend or less.

01Office by office

Where each office stands

Capacity has three components: how much of its own ceiling the office uses, whether demand is arriving fast enough to close the gap unaided, and how much delivered capacity is lost to non-attendance.

The headline percentage on each card is capacity used — the office’s delivered multiple divided by its own ceiling, both printed directly beneath it. Glen Oaks delivers 0.95× against a ceiling of 1.48×, so 0.95 ÷ 1.48 = 64%. Because every office is measured against its own ceiling rather than a shared one, the figures compare like with like even though the ceilings differ.

Part twoHow the numbers were derived

02The ceiling

There is no single ceiling — it depends on block allocation

The utilization column in the export is not a fill rate. Templated hours are wall-clock; scheduled hours are appointment minutes summed across concurrent blocks. Because those are different units, the figure routinely exceeds 100% — so 100% is not the ceiling, and the real ceiling has to be derived.

Capacity is set by how many concurrent blocks a provider’s template carries. New providers schedule single blocks and sit near 1.0×; tenured providers carry double blocks. Triple blocks exist but are rare enough to be immaterial.

Block allocation sets the normal operating level rather than a hard limit — a schedule can be overridden to book concurrently on any given day, and single-block providers do occasionally exceed 1.0×. Because the ceilings below are measured from what providers actually delivered, those override days are already in the data. Using the 95th percentile rather than the maximum keeps an occasional stretch day from being treated as the standard.

So each provider gets their own ceiling, taken as whichever is higher of two figures: what providers with the same block structure typically sustain, or what that provider has themselves proven they can sustain. The result ranges from 0.95× to 1.87×.

The ceiling is a good-day standard: the level a provider reaches on their better days, measured as the 95th percentile of their daily record. It is deliberately a stretch. Headroom throughout this document therefore means what would be gained if every day looked like a good day, which is an upper bound rather than a forecast.

How these numbers are calculated

Every figure comes from four columns in the export — Time Templated, Time Scheduled, Booked Appointments, and Primary Provider — aggregated over the 13 weeks to 8 Aug 2026. Nothing is modelled or estimated except the provider ceilings.

Step 1 · Each provider’s ceiling

Take every day that provider has worked across the full two years and compute Scheduled ÷ Templated for each. A provider whose 99th percentile stays under 1.15 is running single blocks; everyone else has double blocks. Their ceiling is then whichever of two figures is higher: the benchmark for their block tier, or their own 95th percentile. The 95th percentile is used rather than the maximum so that occasional schedule overrides, which let any provider book concurrently on a given day, do not set the sustained ceiling. Note that this is a per-day standard being compared against a 13-week average, so an office at 100% of ceiling would mean every day matching its best days — which is why no office approaches it.

single-block benchmark = 0.95 double-block benchmark = 1.46 (median 90th pct of tenured double-block providers) provider ceiling = max( tier benchmark , provider's own 95th pct ) Giselle peers 1.46 own 0.95 pct 1.17 -> ceiling 1.46 Hyun Soo peers 1.46 own 0.95 pct 1.87 -> ceiling 1.87 Aileen peers 0.95 own 0.95 pct 0.96 -> ceiling 0.96

Step 2 · Office columns

Template hrs/wk = Σ Templated ÷ 13 Delivered = Σ Scheduled ÷ Σ Templated Ceiling = Σ (Templated × provider ceiling) ÷ Σ Templated Capacity used = Delivered ÷ Ceiling Visits/wk = Σ Booked ÷ 13 Headroom = (Ceiling − Delivered) × Template hrs/wk × visits per scheduled hour

Visits per scheduled hour is Σ Booked ÷ Σ Scheduled, and lands at 4.78 network-wide with a standard deviation of 0.26 across all providers — nobody works materially faster or slower, so it can be treated as a constant per office.

Step 3 · Worked example — Glen Oaks

Templated 653.0 hrs ÷ 13 = 50.2 hrs/wk Scheduled 621.8 hrs ÷ 13 = 47.8 hrs/wk Delivered 621.8 ÷ 653.0 = 0.95× Capacity hrs Σ(tmpl × ceiling) = 964.9 Ceiling 964.9 ÷ 653.0 = 1.48× Capacity used 621.8 ÷ 964.9 = 64% Visits 2,876 ÷ 13 = 221/wk Visits per sched hr 2,876 ÷ 621.8 = 4.63 Spare hours (964.9 − 621.8) ÷ 13 = 26.4 hrs/wk Headroom 26.4 × 4.63 = 122 visits/wk

Glen Oaks draws on five providers over the period, four with a 1.50× ceiling and one at 1.71×, weighted by their templated hours — which is how the office arrives at 1.48×. Published figures are rounded, so hand calculations may differ by one or two visits.

Headroom is gross, not bookable tomorrow. These figures assume every day performs like a good day and every spare minute is filled. In practice a new patient consumes 20 minutes and generates roughly four follow-ups, so a full episode costs about 62 minutes. For planning growth, divide headroom by three.

04Forward book

Empty-looking weeks are not spare capacity

Appointments are booked through July 2027, and they contain a trap. Six weeks out every office looks nearly empty — Bronx Concourse shows more open hours than Glen Oaks. Those hours are not headroom; they are demand that has not arrived yet.

This practice books late: 105% of template filled in the final week, 28% at five-to-eight weeks. What distinguishes offices is how fast they fill relative to each other.

This is a moving picture. The forward figures are a snapshot taken 8 Aug 2026 and change daily as appointments are booked, cancelled and rescheduled. Near horizons move fastest — next week’s fill is largely settled, while the 5–8 week band will roughly triple before those dates arrive. Forward numbers are also gross of no-shows and cancellations, so expect them to settle 16–47% lower depending on the office. Treat pace relative to other offices as the durable signal, and re-run before acting on any absolute figure.

05Capacity lost

The largest single block of capacity is already paid for

Across medical, 42% of scheduled appointments end as a no-show or cancellation — about 1,706 slots a week. The rates are not evenly spread: the Bronx offices run no-show rates roughly three times Fort Lee’s, inside the same organisation on the same systems.

06Limits of this assessment

Three things the data cannot settle

  1. When does a provider move to double blocks? The ceilings assume block allocation follows the pattern visible in the data. If the decision is discretionary rather than tenure-based, Bronx Concourse’s ceiling may not rise on its own, and its headroom is smaller than shown.
  2. Is Glen Oaks constrained by catchment or by operations? Low utilisation is consistent with both a thin surrounding population and an under-marketed office. The data cannot distinguish them; ten calls as a prospective patient would.
  3. How much of the cancellation rate is practice-initiated? Cancellations run 19–29% at every office including the best performers, unlike no-shows which vary sharply. A rate that uniform suggests some are clinic reschedules — a scheduling problem rather than a patient one.

07Next phase

What the formal study will measure

This assessment works around a gap. The export aggregates to one row per provider per day, so it holds no record of individual appointment slots — and without slots there is no true fill rate and no third next available. Everything above is built from appointment minutes instead.

The formal study will measure capacity and fill rate directly:

WHAT THIS STUDY USES (appointment minutes, no slot data) delivered multiple = scheduled appointment minutes ÷ templated minutes capacity used = delivered multiple ÷ good-day ceiling WHAT WE WANT (requires a slot-level export) capacity = bookable slots in the template fill rate = booked slots ÷ bookable slots open slots = bookable slots − booked slots 3rd next available = date of the 3rd open slot, by appointment type

One input unlocks all four: an open slot inventory from EMA — one row per slot with date, time, provider, appointment type, and whether it is booked. Practices can usually produce this even when template exports are unavailable, and it is worth requesting before the study begins.

Three additions will need data from outside the scheduling system: demand, counted as appointment requests and referrals rather than only bookings made; the binding constraint at each office, which this study assumed to be the provider and at some sites will not be; and a target utilisation per office, since waiting times rise sharply above roughly 85% and the ceiling is not the goal.

The three open questions in section 06 are the natural starting point, since each requires information gathered outside this export.

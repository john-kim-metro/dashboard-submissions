Metro Dermatology — July 2026 Medical & Productivity Review

Author: Darell Dado · Reporting period: July 2026 · Prepared: August 9, 2026 · Status: Confidential
Companion analysis to the Medical Volume & No-Show and Medical Productivity dashboards

1Per operating-day, visits held flat while demand rose — cancellations are the whole story

Completed medical visits came in at 13,042 in July, down 2.3% from June and 3.2% below July 2025. Total appointment demand, however, rose 0.5% to 22,282. Patients booked at the same rate as June; fewer of those bookings converted into a completed visit.

Almost all of the raw decline is calendar. July carried 23.5 operating-day-equivalents against June’s 24.0 — the practice was closed Friday 3 July (holiday observed, the 4th falling on a Saturday) and Saturday 4 July. Normalising for that, the −2.3% raw fall becomes −0.3% per operating-day, and −3.2% YoY becomes −1.2%. Throughput per day open was essentially unchanged.

That makes the conversion gap sharper, not softer. Demand per operating-day rose 2.6% (924.0 to 948.2) while completed visits per day were flat. More patients wanted appointments on each day the practice was open, and the same number got seen.

Completed medical visits — seasonal trend by year
July 2026 (13,042) sits below July 2025 (13,475) and just above July 2024 (12,764). Raw counts — two fewer operating-day-equivalents than June.

The decomposition is unusually clean. No-show rate was essentially flat at 20.0% (from 20.2%). Cancellation rate rose to 26.8% from 24.6% — the highest month of 2026 and 2.2 points above June. Rates are ratios and are unaffected by the operating-day count, so unlike the volume figures these moves are entirely real. Demand per day rose; cancellations took the difference.

This is a conversion problem, not a demand problem. The distinction matters because the remedies are different. Falling demand calls for marketing and referral work; falling conversion calls for reminder cadence, waitlist backfill, deposit or late-cancel policy, and scheduling-friction review. Normalised for the two lost operating days, throughput per day was flat while demand per day rose 2.6%. Everything that went wrong happened between booking and arrival — and the raw −2.3% headline should not be read as a demand decline.

2Follow-up cancellations at 30.6% are where the volume actually went

Cancellation rate is not evenly distributed. Follow-up — the largest category at 7,276 completed visits — cancelled at 30.6%, the worst of any category and well above the 26.8% system figure. Follow-up also carries a 23.1% no-show rate. Together, close to half of all booked follow-up appointments do not result in a completed visit.

By location the no-show picture is starker than the system average suggests. Bronx Fordham reached 32.2% and Bronx Concourse 27.4%, against Fort Lee at 10.6% and Flushing Northern at 12.8%. A three-fold spread across sites in the same practice is not patient-mix alone.

Follow-up is the highest-leverage target in the practice. It is the largest category and has the worst conversion. A one-point reduction in follow-up cancellation is worth roughly 100 completed visits a month — more than the entire Screening & Diagnostics category. Whether the lever is reminder timing, rescheduling friction, or how far ahead follow-ups are booked is worth establishing before choosing an intervention.

3The Procedures decline is now ten months deep and structural

Procedures held flat month-over-month at 2,028 completed visits, but sits 17.0% below July 2025. The category has now run below its prior-year level for ten consecutive months. Monthly volume has moved from a 2,400–2,600 band through 2024 and early 2025 to roughly 1,950–2,100 today.

Procedures — monthly completed visits (Jan 2024 – Jul 2026)
The step down from the 2024 band is sustained, not a dip.

A decline this persistent is no longer a series of bad months. The previously identified drivers — payer-rule changes affecting laser coverage, and shifting SAAS ordering patterns — would both produce exactly this shape: a level shift rather than a gradual slope. The category is roughly 400 visits a month below where it ran eighteen months ago.

Procedures deserves a decision, not another month of monitoring. If the payer-rule change is permanent, the current level is the new baseline and capacity planning should reflect it. If SAAS ordering patterns are the larger factor, that is addressable clinically. The open drill-down at Fordham and Flushing Northern remains the fastest way to separate the two, and it has now been outstanding for several cycles.

PProvider productivity

This section uses VPH — visits per templated hour — rather than the VPD measure used above. VPD normalizes demand against the calendar; VPH measures efficiency against staffed supply. They can move in opposite directions, and this month they move the same way for a specific reason. Medical only.

System VPH fell to 5.23 as capacity was added into softening demand

System VPH dropped to 5.23 in July from 5.67 in June — a 7.8% fall and the sharpest single-month decline in the two-year window. The cause is visible in the two inputs: templated hours rose 5.0% to 2,086, the highest in the window, while provider visits fell 2.9%.

System VPH and templated hours (Aug 2024 – Jul 2026)
Bars = templated hours (right axis). Line = system VPH (left axis). Dashed = 24-month production average, 5.07.

The hours increase is explainable provider by provider and looks deliberate rather than anomalous: Yu Jin Kim continued ramping (30 to 100 hours), and Ortiz, Garcia and Huang all returned to normal schedules after an unusually light June. This was capacity coming back online, and it arrived in the month demand softened.

Note: a further 68 templated hours appeared against A. Mamanova in the July export. Those were a scheduling error — she returns from leave later in August — and are excluded from hours, VPH and the active-provider count. Left in, they would have understated system VPH at 5.11 and shown 18 active providers rather than 17.

Capacity and demand moved in opposite directions. That is the single most consequential fact in this month’s numbers. VPH at 5.23 is still above the 5.07 two-year average, so this is not yet a crisis — but an 8% efficiency fall in one month, driven by added hours meeting fewer completed visits, compounds quickly if August repeats it. The cancellation problem in Story 1 and this productivity fall are the same problem measured two ways.

Every member of the 2025 cohort fell back this month

All four 2025-cohort providers declined in July, which is unusual enough to be worth separating from individual performance. Given system VPH fell 7.8% on added hours, the cohort moves are largely the same effect rather than four simultaneous individual regressions.

The capacity gap is now concentrated in high no-show panels

The trailing three-month provider matrix separates two distinct problems. The leaky-panel group combines strong throughput with high no-show: Ortiz at 6.75 VPH but 31.6% NS, Shin at 6.80 and 28.7%, Kwak at 4.86 and 27.6%. These providers are working efficiently against a panel that does not turn up. The ramping group — Espinal at 3.20, Garcia at 3.32, Sit at 4.23 — has the opposite profile.

At the other end, Hyun Soo Lee combines the highest VPH in the practice (6.90) with the lowest no-show rate (8.0%), and Stephanie Hu (6.52 VPH, 10.7% NS) and Chohee Park (6.23, 11.8%) sit in the same quadrant. The spread between an 8% and a 32% no-show panel inside one practice is the largest single recoverable opportunity in these numbers.

Two problems, two remedies. High-VPH/high-NS providers do not need throughput coaching — they need panel management: reminder intensity, overbooking policy, waitlist backfill. Ramping providers need the opposite. Applying one remedy to both wastes the effort. Given that system-wide cancellations rather than no-shows drove this month, the panel-management work has just become more urgent than the ramp work.
